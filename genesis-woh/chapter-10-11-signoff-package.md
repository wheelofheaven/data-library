## What's in Chapter 10 (Table of Nations)

The chapter is mostly enumeration — three branches (Yapheth/Ham/Shem) of post-Flood peoples. The lens-relevant material concentrates in two places:

**1. The Nimrod parenthesis (vv 8–12).** The chapter pauses the enumeration to introduce a single figure. *Hu hechel lihyot gibbor ba-aretz* — *he was the first to be a mighty one in the land* — uses the **same noun** גִּבּוֹר *gibbor* that named the antediluvian *gibborim anshei ha-shem* of 6:4 (the offspring of the *bnei ha-elohim*). The verbal echo asserts that the *gibbor*-class did **not** end with the Flood. Nimrod founds the southern Mesopotamian civilization (Bavel, Erech [Uruk], Akkad, Calneh in Shinar) and the northern (Nineveh, Calah, Rehoboth-Ir). The Wheel of Heaven lens reads this as the post-Flood lineage of the *gibborim* surviving in the *gibbor-tzayid* (mighty hunter / founder-king) class.

**2. The Peleg etymology (v 25).** *Peleg* — פֶּלֶג — from the root פלג 'to divide'. *Ki v'yamav niflega ha-aretz* — *for in his days the land was divided*. The commentary lays out three readings (linguistic-political Babel scattering, geological continent-division, post-Flood land-allotment) and lands on the linguistic-political reading as most defensible from the Hebrew lexicon and the chapter's own self-frame.

The closing summary (v32) names the chapter's keyword: *separation* (*niphredu*).

## What's in Chapter 11 (Babel + line to Abram)

Three distinct units in one chapter:

**1. The Babel narrative (vv 1–9).** The highest-lens-leverage section in the chapter. Key features the translation surfaces:

- **The doubled unity claim (v1).** *Safa achat udvarim achadim* — *one lip and one set of words* — preserved as the doubled phrase the Hebrew has (against the standard English smoothing to 'one language and one speech').

- **The cohortative quartet.** Two human cohortatives — *havah nilbenah* (v3 'let us make bricks') and *havah nivneh* (v4 'let us build') — mirrored by two divine cohortatives — *havah neredah* (v7 'let us go down') and *navlah* (v7 'let us confuse'). The chapter is structurally built around this rhetorical match. The translation preserves the cohortative form in all four cases.

- **`Naase lanu shem` (v4).** Human cohortative using the **same form** as the divine *naase adam* of Gen 1:26. The chapter is staging humans assuming the divine cohortative register; the verbal parallel is exact. The commentary surfaces the parallel; the translation preserves the bare cohortative.

- **`Vayered YHWH` (v5).** YHWH *came down* — preserved as anthropomorphic descent-language, mirroring the tower's ascent.

- **`Lo yibatzer mehem` (v6).** The chapter's load-bearing claim: *nothing will be withheld from them which they have schemed to do*. The verb *yibatzer* names the divine recognition that human coordination at scale produces unlimited capacity. Not curse-language but engineering-assessment-language. The lens reads this as the Elohim recognizing the capacity threat of unified human technological coordination; the response in v7 is a targeted cap, not a punishment.

- **The third explicit divine plural cohortative (v7).** After 1:26 (*naase adam*) and 3:22 (*k'achad mimennu*), the third instance: *havah neredah v'navlah* — *come, let us go down and let us confuse*. The chapter has now established a pattern of plural-divine speech at three key moments in primaeval history.

- **The reversal completed (v8).** What the humans feared in v4 (*pen-naputz* — 'lest we be scattered') is what YHWH does in v8 (*vayyafetz YHWH otam* — 'YHWH scattered them'). The same root in passive (human fear) and causative (divine action).

- **The Bavel/balal pun (v9).** The Mesopotamian self-naming *Bab-ilim* ('gate of the gods') is recoded by Hebrew folk-etymology as 'the place of confusion' (*balal*). Theological re-naming.

**2. The Shem-to-Abram genealogy (vv 10–26).** Ten generations, paralleling chapter 5's ten generations from Adam to Noah. Lifespans drop from Shem's 600 to Terah's 205 — the antediluvian-scale lifespans are gone. The genealogy lands at Abram in v26.

**3. The Terah household and the unfinished migration (vv 27–32).** Sarai's barrenness (v30) names the constraint the next ten chapters of Genesis will work to overcome. Terah leaves Ur Kasdim toward Canaan with Abram, Lot, and Sarai — but stops at Charan and dies there. Chapter 12 will open with YHWH's call to Abram to complete the migration his father had abandoned.

## Differences from prior chapters (Gen 1–9) worth flagging

1. **Glossary growth rate.** v1.0 → v2.0 across 9 chapters was ~147 entries (~16/chapter). v2.0 → v2.1 across 2 chapters added 25 entries (~12.5/chapter). The growth rate is sustainable; the central glossary is not bloating.

2. **Commentary style.** Chapters 10–11 commentary is somewhat longer per verse than chapters 5–7 (the prior genealogy-heavy chapters), reflecting the higher density of lens-relevant material in the Nimrod parenthesis and Babel narrative. The commentary register and citation style match the established Editor convention.

3. **Toponym/ethnonym handling.** The chapter follows the established convention of preserving Hebrew forms (Bavel, Mitsrayim, Pelishtim, Kasdim) over English-traditional forms (Babylon, Egypt, Philistines, Chaldeans). Where the English-traditional form is *close to* the Hebrew (Babel, Canaan, Shinar), the English-traditional form is used. **One inconsistency worth noting:** Abram is rendered as "Abram" (English convention) not "Avram" (Hebrew form). The convention is documented in the `terach-avram-nachor-haran` glossary entry's rationale and deferred for a future glossary decision.
